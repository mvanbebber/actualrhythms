How it compares

The only rhythm trainer built from real music.

Rhythm-reading apps make up their exercises: a generator strings note values together, or the developer writes drills. Actual Rhythms doesn't make up a single one. Every rhythm is a passage from a real piece, credited to its source, and every tap you make is timed against it.

What "only" means here

In November 2026 we searched the App Store, Google Play and the web for rhythm-reading trainers. Every one we found either generates its rhythms, ships its own drills, or works on scores you import yourself. None has a built-in library of real music to read from and tap. If one exists, we couldn't find it. Apps change, so the table below says what each one's own listing or website said when we checked, with links.

The largest library of rhythms

Actual Rhythms has 75,977 distinct rhythms, more than 100 times the largest fixed set we found in another trainer (700+ in Rhythm Trainer – Tap and Read; 252 in Complete Rhythm Trainer; about 240 in Rhythm Sight Reading Trainer). Generators can produce exercises without end, but every one of them is made up. Here every rhythm was played or written by someone first.

Why it matters

A generator can't tell you what music sounds like.

Real frequencies

A random generator gives a rare figure the same chance as a common one. In real music, two bars of steady quarters in 4/4 appear in 505 different pieces, and the dotted eighth, sixteenth and two eighths of a 2/4 march (written six different ways) in 206. You meet the figures you'll meet on the stand.

Real phrasing

Composers and players leave rests where a singer breathes, push into the downbeat, and end phrases on long notes. A string of random note values has none of that shape. A passage from a real piece always does.

Real context

Every rhythm comes with its piece, its player, its year and its melody. You can hear what you just read, and see the same figure in a Mozart quartet, a Scottish reel and a bebop solo.
